\section{Introduction}

In transportation research, points of interest (POIs) and residential
locations are routinely used as origins and destinations when
estimating travel demand and modelling travel behaviour. The
underlying data are drawn from a variety of sources---travel surveys,
property registries, GPS traces, and, increasingly OpenStreetMap,
which serves both as a crowdsourced database and as a common,
openly licensed container into which licensed-compatible authoritative
datasets can be imported and harmonised in a single consistent
format. When building footprints are available in OSM, most
researchers approximate each POI by the centroid of its polygon (or
of the parcel when no building is mapped). For small, single-purpose
buildings this is usually acceptable; for larger structures and
multi-building sites such as university campuses, hospital grounds,
shopping centres or industrial complexes, the centroid can
misrepresent the actual access point by hundreds of
metres---sometimes kilometres---a distance that is not negligible for
walking or first-/last-mile transit access.

Entrances can be represented explicitly in OSM through the
\texttt{entrance=*} tag (\texttt{main}, \texttt{customers},
\texttt{home}, \texttt{yes}, and others), but they remain sparsely
mapped outside a few well-covered regions. Large sites typically have
several entrances with distinct functions---main, visitors,
employees, students, deliveries, emergency---each with its own
accessibility profile by mode and user group. Precise access-point
modelling is particularly critical for paratransit and wheelchair
users, elderly travellers or those carrying luggage, transit riders
for whom the stop-to-door segment is a significant share of total
travel time, and cyclists seeking secure parking. It also matters
for routing engines---including the emerging fleet of autonomous
taxis, for which curbside drop-off precision is a safety and
usability requirement.

A growing body of work characterises the completeness and positional
accuracy of OSM buildings and POIs against authoritative and
commercial sources (Haklay, 2010; Girres \& Touya, 2010; Fan et al.,
2014; Herfort et al., 2023; Moradi et al., 2023; Klinkhardt et al.,
2023), and shows that entrance- or stop-to-door-level detail changes
transit catchments (Gutiérrez \& García-Palomares, 2008; Biba et al.,
2010; El-Geneidy et al., 2014), wheelchair routing (Neis \& Zielstra,
2014) and curbside pick-up/drop-off outcomes for autonomous vehicles
(Hunter et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). The \texttt{entrance=*}
tag itself, however, remains a blind spot: Hu et al. (2021) motivated
a machine-learning inference approach by noting that only about 60
buildings in their entire London extract carried an entrance tag.
Our prior work (Bourbonnais et al., 2026) manually validated 1{,}034
POIs from five Québec household travel surveys and observed median
positional errors around 20m across OSM, Overture Maps, the Québec
Property Registry and Google Places, with long tails dominated by
university campuses and hospital complexes, and explicitly flagged
the comparison of validated entrances against building and parcel
centroids as open work. To our knowledge, no study yet quantifies
\texttt{entrance=*} coverage at regional or global scale, nor the
effect of substituting a centroid for an entrance on
transport-analysis outcomes.

This study aims to quantify the impact of entrance-level detail in
OSM on accessibility and travel-demand metrics, to compare OSM's
positional representation of POIs with that of major commercial and
authoritative datasets, and to demonstrate how entrances can be
mapped and tagged in OSM to support walking, cycling, transit and
emerging autonomous-vehicle use cases.

\section{Methodology}

The study combines a global exploratory analysis with a local,
in-depth case study.

At the global level, a two-stage population-weighted sample is drawn.
First, 10 × 10 km bounding boxes are selected from a global population
raster (GHS-POP from the Joint Research Centre, or the Kontur
Population H3 grid) with probability proportional to
population, so that the spatial sample reflects where transport
activity actually occurs rather than land area; a secondary
stratification ensures representation of small single-purpose
buildings alongside larger structures and multi-building sites such as
university campuses, hospital grounds, shopping centres and industrial
complexes. Within each bounding box, a POI is drawn uniformly at
random from an Overpass query over the relevant OSM tag classes and
classified along four dimensions: (i) presence or absence of a building footprint; (ii)
single-building versus multi-building site; (iii) presence of explicit
\texttt{entrance=*} nodes; and (iv) specificity of the entrance tag
(i.e.\ values other than \texttt{yes}, such as \texttt{main},
\texttt{customers}, \texttt{home}, \texttt{service} or
\texttt{emergency}). Reference entrance locations are established
from a combination of open and proprietary street-level imagery
(Mapillary, KartaView, Panoramax, Google Street View) and, where
publicly available, architectural or wayfinding plans linked from OSM
or the web. Where a building
footprint is present, three candidate reference points are computed
and compared: the centroid of the underlying parcel, the centroid of
the main building, and the centroid of the multipolygon formed by all
buildings on the site. Each candidate---as well as every
OSM-tagged entrance---is then evaluated against the reference
entrances through a set of accessibility-relevant metrics: Euclidean
distance, network-based walking distance and its tortuosity (ratio of
network to straight-line distance), and distance to the nearest
transit stop, cycle-parking or bike-share dock, off-street parking
lot, and legal drop-off location for a vehicle.

At the local level, an in-depth analysis is conducted on approximately
100 POIs in the province of Québec for which entrances were surveyed
and contributed to OSM by the Chaire Mobilité team. In addition to
the metrics above, these sites are compared against POI locations
from leading commercial and open providers---Google, Apple,
Overture Maps---as well as the provincial property registry,
quantifying positional differences between
OSM, authoritative and commercial sources. Systematically comparing
OSM against proprietary imagery and commercial POI datasets is a
deliberate methodological choice: quantifying the gap between the two
is what enables an evidence-based case for targeted OSM improvements
and for the release of authoritative data by public administrations.

The analysis pipeline uses the Overpass API for OSM data extraction,
PostGIS for spatial integration, and QGIS for inspection; an
interactive MapLibre-based viewer is developed to explore individual
sites and to communicate results. Because many of the short access
paths connecting entrances to adjacent sidewalks, transit stops,
parking areas or drop-off zones are not themselves mapped in OSM,
network distances are computed with a custom routing procedure that
complements the OSM network where necessary. All extraction and
analysis code is released under an open-source licence, and the
sampled POI list, reference entrance locations and computed metrics
are published alongside the paper to support reproduction and
extension.
